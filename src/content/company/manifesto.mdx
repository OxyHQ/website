<Takeaways>

## Summary

Most of what is wrong with modern software is not a bug. It is a business model working exactly as designed. When the person using a product is not the person paying for it, every decision drifts toward whoever is: attention gets harvested, data accumulates, and leaving quietly gets harder each year. Nobody has to be a villain for that to happen. A metric and enough time will do it.

Oxy is an attempt to prove the other path is real. Not a manifesto about ethics attached to an ordinary company, but products whose architecture makes the promises checkable: identity you hold on your own device, source anyone can read, open protocols that let you leave, and a revenue model with no reason to keep you scrolling.

This page is the short version. It says what we believe and what we refuse. The [Founding Charter](/company/charter) is the long one: what Oxy is trying to become, what it must protect while it grows, and what should be hardest to change.

## 1. It started as a refusal

At twelve years old, Nate Isern Álvarez looked at what had become normal online, the ads, the data collection, the artificial restrictions, and concluded that digital life did not have to work that way. There was no business plan. There was an attempt to build a better social network.

The project kept growing because the original problem was never isolated. The same pattern showed up in housing, in music, in commerce, in public conversation, in the tools people use to work: technology treating people as inventory, markets rewarding extraction, and platforms concentrating power in the hands of whoever owns the servers. Oxy stopped being one app and became a response to the pattern.

We have a company because servers, contracts, employment and responsibility require one. The corporate form is the instrument, not the point.

## 2. The pattern, stated plainly

### 2.1 Attention became the inventory

A feed tuned for engagement is not neutral about your time, and it is very good at its job. It finds the specific thing that keeps you past the point where you would have chosen to stop. That is not a discipline problem on your side. It is the result of a controlled experiment run on millions of people to find what works on you.

### 2.2 Your history became someone else's asset

Your messages, photos, work, social graph and the decade of context that makes them meaningful sit in databases you cannot read, under terms that can be revised by email. When a service shuts down, gets acquired, or decides your account broke a rule it will not explain, most exports are technically compliant and practically useless.

### 2.3 Leaving got expensive on purpose

Lock in is rarely announced. It accumulates: an identity that exists only inside one company, contacts that do not travel, formats nothing else can read, a cancellation flow with six screens. Loyalty that depends on the cost of leaving is not loyalty. It is a hostage situation with a good design system.

### 2.4 Why the usual corrections do not reach it

Regulation sets a floor, and the floor matters, but it mostly governs disclosure rather than architecture: the same collection continues, now with a banner in front of it. Privacy features inside an extractive product are real and bounded, because the boundary is always drawn where it would start costing revenue. And moving to a smaller company helps right up until that company needs to grow and rediscovers why the incumbents behave the way they do. What is left is the part all three route around: who holds the keys, who can read the code, and where the money comes from.

## 3. What we are building instead

### 3.1 An identity you actually hold

Oxy ID is the account you use across every Oxy app, and the thing that proves it lives on your device rather than in our database. You authenticate by proving possession of it, not by asking us to vouch for you.

Three consequences follow, and all three are the point. We cannot impersonate you, because we do not hold what would be needed. A breach of our infrastructure is not a breach of your identity, because the useful material was never there. And moving to a new device is a transfer you perform, which is why recovery gets real engineering attention instead of a support queue.

### 3.2 One institution, many doors

Commons and Accounts hold identity. Mention is an open social space connected to the wider fediverse through ActivityPub. Allo handles communication, Syra music, Mercaria buying and selling, Homiio housing, CrowdSource participatory moderation, and Alia, Codea, Astro and Oxy OS pursue independence in AI, code, browsing and personal computing.

These are not separate brands competing for the same attention. They share identity, infrastructure and values, so the work that makes one product good makes the others better, and there is one place to audit rather than eight. You might arrive as a listener, a tenant, a seller or a developer. The invitation underneath is the same.

### 3.3 Open by default, because it is the only checkable part

If we can open it, we open it: source, APIs, protocols, design system, infrastructure. Not as a badge, but because it is the mechanism that turns a claim into something you can verify. If we say a key never leaves your device, that claim lives in code you can read. If we say a request carries no identifier, you can watch the request.

Publishing also changes what we are able to do later. Code thousands of people can read cannot quietly grow a tracker, and a protocol other people implement cannot be silently broken in our favour.

### 3.4 Federation over a walled garden

Where an open protocol exists, we speak it. Someone on a different server, running different software, operated by people we have never met, is a first class participant.

By conventional measures that is worse for us. It caps network effects, complicates moderation and hands part of the experience to operators we do not control. We do it because the alternative is the exact structure that makes every later abuse possible. If leaving is cheap, we have to keep earning the stay.

## 4. What is not for sale

Some decisions can change as technology, culture and law change. These cannot, and they are written down so that breaking them is visible.

- **Human dignity.** A person's worth is not their account, income, productivity or reputation. Safety and moderation decisions may limit features when necessary, but they stay proportionate, explainable and open to review. Dignity is not a score we grant.
- **Privacy.** We do not sell personal data, trade it for advantage, or repurpose it for behavioural advertising. We collect less, keep it for less time, and put control in your hands.
- **No advertising based extraction.** We do not finance core services by selling access to human attention, and we do not build engagement systems whose purpose is keeping you present for someone else's benefit.
- **Meaningful access.** A free tier should be genuinely useful, not a preview engineered to create dependency. Paid plans add capacity and tools with real costs. Payment is never the price of dignity, fair treatment, or leaving with your data.
- **Honest design.** No dark patterns, hidden fees, false scarcity, manufactured urgency or cancellation mazes. Consent means something only when refusing is possible and understandable.
- **Open source and transparency.** Code that affects the public interest is open by default where privacy, security and law allow, along with the rules, architecture and limitations needed for informed trust.
- **Freedom to connect and to leave.** Open protocols, interoperability and portability, so nothing about your identity, relationships or history traps you.
- **Power with limits.** Nobody holds unreviewable authority over the rights, speech, identity or livelihood of others here. Not the founder, not investors, not employees, not automated systems.

## 5. Money, without making you the product

A mission that cannot pay for infrastructure, security, maintenance and people ends up dependent on someone whose interests are not yours. So Oxy earns money on purpose: premium plans, managed hosting, support and professional tools, institutional plans, clearly disclosed fees, ethical commerce, licensing consistent with our open source commitments, grants and research partnerships.

The test is never whether money changes hands. It is whether the exchange is clear, voluntary, proportionate and connected to real value or real cost. We do not create an obstacle and then charge you to remove it, and we do not turn safety, privacy or ordinary respect into premium features.

Capital is welcome on the same terms. It may buy an economic interest. It may not buy the right to require advertising, the sale of personal data, manipulative growth or the removal of open source commitments. Money may sustain the mission. It may not own it.

## 6. Built for fallible people

We assume every leader is fallible, including the one who started this. A promise that a founder will always behave well is not a safeguard for millions of people. It is a hope.

So the rules that affect you should be public and applied consistently, important decisions should come with reasons, and anyone affected should have a real appeal rather than a form that goes nowhere. Decisions that turn out wrong get corrected in the open instead of quietly rewritten.

As Oxy grows, governance has to include the people who use the services, the people who build them, contributors and independent stewards. Constitutional questions, privacy, identity, funding, community rights, deserve a higher bar than ordinary product choices, and should never rest with one executive.

CrowdSource is our first serious attempt at that shape: reports become cases, evidence is preserved, a temporary jury drawn at random reviews it, and the decision is versioned and published. Appeals seat a new jury rather than erase the first record.

## 7. How to hold us to it

Read the source. Watch the requests. Export everything and go somewhere else, then come back if we earn it. Tell us publicly when a product contradicts something on this page, and name the decision rather than the vibe.

We will get things wrong. Some of this will look naive in five years, and some of it we will fail under pressure we have not met yet. The useful response is specific and public, which is why the commitments above are written as refusals: a principle only becomes credible when violating it is difficult, visible and costly.

## Our pledge

<Takeaways>

- Build alternatives, not slogans.
- Protect the person before the product.
- Make money without making people the product.
- Share power before power has to be taken away.
- Grow without forgetting why the work began.
- Leave behind an institution that helps people live with greater dignity than the systems it replaced.

</Takeaways>
